### Part One: The Callout

Cole Porter was a grid contractor. For twenty-two years, when something broke under Haven City that Seraph's repair units couldn't reach or couldn't figure out, the dispatch system sent Cole. Old junctions. Pre-Seraph pipework. The parts of the city that were older than the god that ran it.

He carried municipal certification, a diagnostic lens over his left eye that read grid signatures through walls, and a paper notebook in his chest pocket with twenty-two years of jobs in it, written in pencil.

The younger contractors laughed at the notebook. Everything was supposed to be logged to the grid, and Cole logged to the grid like everyone else. But he wrote it down too, because the grid's records had started coming back wrong. Nine glitches that month. Work orders with addresses he didn't remember. Invoices that changed size overnight. The records belonged to Seraph, and Seraph had been getting things wrong.

Paper didn't glitch. That was the reason for the notebook.

He'd slept badly the night before, which he only mentioned to himself because it was the dream again. He'd been having it on and off for three years. A white ceiling. Someone humming, low and even. A woman's voice, very gentle, saying something he could never hold onto after he woke. It wasn't a nightmare. That was the strange part. He always woke from it calm, and the calm always felt like it belonged to somebody else.

The callout came at the fourth evening bell, in the middle of a stutter, which told him it was bad. Dispatch didn't interrupt a glitch for small things.

He read it twice. He knew Block 44's sub-level. He'd done a valve job down there five or six years ago, and there wasn't enough pipe down there to lose forty percent of anything.

The corridor lights were still at half power when he shouldered his kit. Up and down the street, doors were wedged open and windows were cracked. Nobody had told anyone to do it. The block just did it now, the way you close shutters before a storm. The air had the thin, flat taste it got when the atmospheric processors fell behind. Fourth stutter that week. People barely looked up from their dinners anymore.

The access shaft behind the water reclamation building took his code on the third try. He clipped his lamp to his shoulder and went down the ladder into the dark under Block 44.

His boots hit standing water at the bottom. Two inches and rising.

### Part Two: Below

The sub-level was a service gallery, pipe and conduit running along both walls. His lamp lit a long cone that ended in black. The lens overlaid the grid map on his left eye, water lines in blue, power in yellow, every piece of the city's body tagged and numbered.

The water was coming from the north end. He waded toward it, and the pressure readout dropped another point while he walked.

He found the breach at the gallery's north wall, and he stood there longer than he should have, because it didn't make sense.

The main had split at a junction collar. That part was ordinary. But the water wasn't coming out of the wall. It was coming out of a pipe that ran *through* the wall, a heavy feeder line entering the gallery from the north, and when Cole put his lens on it, the lens drew nothing. No blue line. No tag. No number. The map showed the gallery ending at solid foundation three meters ahead of him.

The pipe came out of a wall the map said was solid, carrying water the grid said didn't exist.

He wiped the lens and rebooted it. While it came back up he kept his bare eye on the pipe.

The map redrew. Solid wall. No pipe.

Cole knew every kind of map error there was. Stale surveys. Scrambled tags. Pre-Seraph pipe that had never been logged right. This wasn't any of them. Unlogged pipe showed up as a gray hash, the grid's way of saying it saw something it couldn't name. This pipe wasn't unnamed. It was missing. The map wasn't saying *I can't read that*. The map was saying *there is nothing there*, and it was saying it with a god's full confidence.

He clamped the breach. It took forty minutes and the water numbed his hands, and the pressure gauge told him something he didn't like the whole time: the feeder line beyond the wall was drawing steady, metered flow. Not a leak. A supply. Somebody's supply.

When the clamp held, he walked the wall.

The lens said foundation stone. His hands found the seam anyway, four meters east of the pipe, under sixty years of paint and grime. The outline of a maintenance door, sealed so long ago the hinges had been painted into the wall.

He stood there with his hand on the warm door and thought about the dream. He didn't know why. The white ceiling, the humming, the voice. It came up out of nowhere, the way a smell brings something back, and it was gone again just as fast.

He took the pry bar off his kit. Opening sealed doors wasn't his job, and he was three years from a pension, and he did it anyway, and if you'd asked him why, the honest answer was that the map had lied to him and he wanted to know what it was lying about.

### Part Three: The Block That Wasn't

Light. That was the first thing. He'd braced for a dead space, an old cistern, a void. Instead there were strings of small white lamps running away from him down a corridor, wired clean, spliced by somebody who knew what they were doing. The corridor opened out thirty meters on, and Cole stopped in the mouth of it and looked at a street.

A street. Underground, low-ceilinged, but a street. Some old sub-level block from before a rebuild nobody remembered, and people lived in it. Laundry hung on lines between the support columns. There was a garden of pale vegetables under grow lamps, planted in beds built out of paving stones. His water, the city's water, ran into a cistern with a valve rig on it that was better work than half the municipal jobs he'd seen. Twenty or thirty small dwellings with curtains for doors. Somewhere, someone was cooking onions.

A dozen people stood in the street looking at him. Human and Valari both, and one Synthetic by the cistern with a wrench in its hand. Nobody ran. Nobody shouted. ### Part Four: The White Room

Cole came up the ladder at the second morning bell. The stutter had passed. The lights were full, the air tasted like air, and the city was doing what it always did after a glitch, which was pretend nothing had happened.

He filed the repair from the corner terminal. The grid took the clamp job, logged his hours, and added a line to the work order:

CAUSE: PRESSURE ANOMALY, SOURCE UNRESOLVED. SUB-LEVEL SURVEY: NO ADDITIONAL STRUCTURES PRESENT.

He read that line twice. Somewhere under his boots there was a street with laundry on lines and a chalk drawing and thirty people cooking onions, and the memory of the whole world said: nothing there.

He walked home. He sat down at his kitchen table in the gray early light and took out the notebook to write it down, because the grid couldn't hold this, and his book could.

The pencil was in his hand when he noticed a dog-eared page further back. He didn't dog-ear pages.

He turned to it. Three years old, between a filter job and a conduit rebuild. His own handwriting. His own pencil.

*Block 44 sub-level. Door in the north wall past the junction. Warm. There's a street behind it. People. Man with a book took my name. Said forget the door.*

And under that, underlined twice, pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper:

He had no memory of writing it. He read it four times and nothing came. Not the door, not the street, not the man. Three years gone, clean.

The white ceiling.

It arrived all at once, the way the dream never did, whole and sharp, and for the first time in three years he could hold onto it. He wasn't dreaming it. He was remembering it.

A room with white tile walls and a white ceiling and light that came from everywhere, so there were no shadows in it at all. Figures stood along the wall in desert-gray, four of them, hoods up, and they moved together, when one shifted its weight the others shifted, like they were all hearing the same slow song. She had her palm flat on his forehead, and her hand was cool and dry, and she was looking at him with what he could only ever describe afterward as kindness, real kindness, the kind you couldn't fake, and that was the most frightening thing he had ever seen.

WELLNESS DISCHARGE. PORTER, C. VOLUNTARY RESTORATIVE STAY, 3 DAYS. SPONSOR: SECTOR REFERRAL. CONDITION ON RELEASE: RESTED.

### Part One: The Night Before

Rainbow had run The Prism for nineteen years and he knew, within thirty seconds of someone walking through the door, exactly what kind of trouble they were.

He was not a small man and The Prism was not a small bar, but the hat made him impossible to miss regardless. Wide-brimmed, spiked crown, nineteen years of patches and pins and small tokens from people who had needed somewhere to put something and chosen his hat as the place. His coat was every color at once, layered and heavy, built up over the same nineteen years the way reefs were built, one piece at a time. His left eye was his own. His right eye had belonged to a Bone Patrol officer until Absalom decided Rainbow needed a reminder about the cost of harboring Unbound, and Rainbow had kept it because it was better than the alternative and because he had decided a long time ago not to hide what the city had done to him. The cane was for posture. He'd been told that. He didn't entirely believe it.

The Prism sat at the end of the approach road to the Tower gate, which meant everyone who registered in the morning walked past it, and most of them found their way inside the night before. Rainbow had learned to read registration eve the way a sailor read weather. By the time both teams had come and gone he'd have a picture of each of them clear enough to last.

Not in the dark, not blind, not after the Tower rearranged itself. It made him the best Sherpa money could contract, and it made Vesper Quill the holder of his contract, and it made him, on nights like this, a man who watched the door and kept his own quiet count of who came through it. He was one of the touched, the ones the city called the Ascended, and like most of them he wore it quietly. You had to know what he was to see it.

"Busy tomorrow?" Rainbow asked him, early, before the teams came in.

"Two teams, one morning." Sable turned his glass a quarter turn. "Somebody's going to want eyes on that."

Marcus's team came in together, by the front door, a few minutes after the tenth bell.

### Part Two: The Oath of Ascent

The Tower ground plaza held three hundred people on a quiet week.

This was not a quiet week.

Rainbow stood near the back of the crowd in his coat and his hat and watched Haven City compress itself into the space before the registration wall. Bone Patrol on the perimeter, four of them, chrome skull-faces and red optics and hammers loose at their sides. Thorne's people somewhere in the crowd, invisible the way Thorne's people were always invisible, present only as the quality of attention that shifted when you moved near them. On every building face in the plaza the screens had already switched to the registration feed. You couldn't escape the Oath of Ascent in Haven City if you tried. Seraph made sure of that. The questions belonged to the city the moment they left a climber's mouth.

Unit-9911 stood at the front of the ceremony in its dark hooded robe, hands pressed together at the chest, the single ring of pale light at its forehead the only bright thing about it. It had been presiding over the Oath of Ascent for as long as Rainbow could remember, and it carried that history in its posture, a stillness that was not indifference. It had watched hundreds of people speak their questions into stone, and it stood like something that remembered all of them.

Beside the registration wall stood the Questron. Blue suit, faceted dark head, the gold question mark centered on its face neither glowing nor dim but simply there, permanent, a face built for one purpose. There were Questrons throughout Haven City and inside the Tower itself, identical in appearance, each one an interface between Seraph and anyone who needed to know something about the climb. They could tell you how many climbers were active in the Tower, what questions had been asked, how long a specific climber had been ascending, what the rules were, what the Oath Lords had done. They answered because Seraph answered through them, and they kept no secrets that Seraph did not want kept. This one's function today was intake: record each name, record each question, verify each registration, ensure the wall received what it was owed. It had no ceremony in it. That was Unit-9911's work. The Questron waited with its hands at its sides and said nothing until it was needed.

The ceremony had no fanfare. That was one of the things Rainbow had always respected about it.

"Eight hundred and forty-seven climbers have reached the peak of the Tower," Unit-9911 said, into the microphones that carried its voice to every screen in the city. "Ten of them swore the Oath of the Lords and were remade into something the city could not ignore. Twenty-seven climbers are active in the Tower at this moment. What became of the others is not recorded here. Many who failed the climb still walk among you, carrying something the Tower gave them that they cannot put down. You may have seen them. You know the ones."

A pause. The crowd was very quiet.

"Who will join them?"

A traditional Valari name was not a preference. It was a lineage. It was everyone who had come before, carried forward. To say it aloud at the registration wall of Haven City and exchange it for something self-invented was not a choice any Valari made. There was no precedent for it. There was no framework for it. Every Valari in the plaza understood, from the first syllable, exactly what had just been discarded.

"The Tower is open," it said again, softly, and the words meant something different now.

### Part Three: The Dispersal

Rainbow was watching the screens cycle back to standard feed when he saw the men who did not belong.

Four of them, moving through a crowd that was still standing stunned, with the coordinated looseness that civilians did not have. Plainclothes, professional. Their spacing kept them from reading as a group to anyone not looking for a group. They were oriented toward Signal, and they had the specific focus of people who had been waiting for a particular moment and were now in it, and the moment they had chosen was this one: three hundred people rooted to the ground by a god's announcement, cover so perfect that no planner could have arranged it.

Rainbow did not move. He never moved until the odds were clear.

Signal saw them approximately two seconds after Rainbow did. It turned toward the nearest operative and its posture changed, all at once and completely, the way a machine's posture changed.

Marcus's eye saw them before his brain finished the calculation. Rainbow could tell by the way the man's hand moved first and then stopped, caught and held back, a half-second argument between the eye and the man. The man won. Barely.

The operatives did not stop at Signal's team.

This was not a retrieval operation. This was a sweep. And Rainbow, watching it unfold in the plaza where a god had just named a prize, understood that Korinth had known. Not the words, maybe. But something. You did not brief a sweep team on all six climbers of two unrelated teams unless the asset you wanted had become part of something larger, and thirty seconds ago the whole city had learned what the something larger was.

The first operative reached Signal and tried to take it by the arm. Signal let him get the grip before it moved, a controlled redirect that sent the man into the second operative with considerable force. Marcus put himself between two more and the crowd, the eye tracking, and his hands found angles that civilians didn't have. Spark engaged the operative on her flank with the mechanical arm and a tool Rainbow couldn't name but didn't need to. Kimo's blade cut light across the plaza stones.

"KI series!" the fourth operative shouted, backing toward the perimeter, a comm unit already at his face. "Confirmed location, registration plaza, requesting immediate support!"

That was the whole of the explanation. Korinth didn't need more than a chassis model and a grid coordinate. The rest of the sweep was apparently just convenience.

Rainbow had stood in front of that gate more times than he could count. It had always been open. It required only that you want to go through, and most people, when they stood in front of it, found out that wanting was the hardest part.

The six of them crossed the plaza toward it at a run, the second wave closing behind them. The civilian operatives slowed at the approach to the gate perimeter. They didn't stop. They were professionals. But they slowed, and the slowing was not a tactical decision. It was the deep, involuntary response of people who had grown up in Haven City and absorbed what the Tower meant through years of watching people walk through that gate and not come back. The gate was open and the gate had always been open and walking through it was a choice and the choice was permanent.

The six climbers did not slow.

The gate took them and they were gone, the gold light closing behind the last of them like a curtain, and then there was only the gate standing open in the morning air, burning, as it always burned, as if waiting for the next person to decide.

The second wave stopped at the perimeter. Stood there for a moment. Then the senior operative said something into his comm and they began to reorganize, because you could not follow registered climbers into the Tower and Korinth, whatever else it was, understood the rules of Haven City.

Rainbow turned and walked back toward The Prism by the long route.

### Part Four: The Screens Go Dark

He was halfway down the approach road when the surveillance node above the transit hub across the street went dark.

Not a power failure. The node had been running cleanly when he passed it on the way to the plaza. Now it was dark, and a thin thread of smoke rose from its housing, and as Rainbow watched, a hairline fracture appeared in the mounting bracket above it and began working its way through what should have been solid-bonded steel.

A section of paving fifteen feet ahead of him shifted, a barely perceptible subsidence, the kind of thing that happened when something underground settled. Except nothing underground should be settling here. This block had been resurfaced two years ago, per Seraph's infrastructure schedule, and Seraph's infrastructure schedules did not produce settling pavement two years later.

Rainbow stopped walking and looked at the paving and then at the node and then at the crack that was still working its way through the mounting bracket.

He had heard about the new Oath Lord. Grey, they called him. The Architect. The fastest climber in the Tower's history, forty-seven days start to finish, a number that had made every faction in Haven City sit up and recalculate, because forty-seven days suggested either extraordinary capability or extraordinary assistance and nobody could agree on which. Rainbow had his own opinion. You didn't write the code that built a god and then have that god make your climb harder than it needed to be. Seraph had reasons for what Seraph did, and Grey's reasons and Seraph's reasons had apparently pointed in the same direction. And now, fifteen months after the city gained its tenth lord, the god was announcing its eleventh and calling it the last. Rainbow did not believe in coincidences that large. He didn't know what the connection was. He knew there was one.

The mounting bracket gave way. The surveillance node dropped eight feet and caught on its cable housing and hung there, swinging slightly, dead.

Rainbow looked at it for a moment and then walked back into The Prism and locked the door behind him.

He stood behind the bar in the quiet of the morning and thought about the six people he had just watched walk into the Tower, into a climb that had meant one thing when they planned it and meant something else entirely by the time the gate closed behind them.

He thought about Nyx'ara first, because she had left the full glass on the table and that was the thing that stayed with him, the glass that nobody had touched. The Valari woman with her circuits suppressed low and her hands flat on the table and the grief that had come from a generation before her and lived in her body anyway. She was climbing to restore the Hive Mind. Rainbow had lived beside the Valari Quarter for nineteen years. He didn't fully understand what the Hive Mind meant to them, but he understood enough. Her question was large. The questions that came from that kind of grief were always large, and they were also almost never the real question, which was something the Tower had a way of making clear.

He thought about Signal, the Korinth chassis with Nathan Zhao inside it, and the bulletin he had been watching for three months on the commercial feed. Korinth wanted the chassis back badly enough to run a sweep at a public ceremony. That was before a god put a throne on the table. Rainbow thought about what a company like Korinth did with the knowledge that the last seat in existence had just been opened, and that their runaway asset was inside the Tower climbing toward it, and he decided the bounty number he'd memorized was about to become a historical footnote.

He came back to Marcus the way he had kept coming back to him all night. The eye that wouldn't stop. The five words at the wall. The way Marcus had stood there, like a man reporting for duty. Asking a god for help was easier for a man like that if he treated it as a mission.

How do I make it stop.

Five words. Rainbow had heard a hundred questions at a hundred registration ceremonies. He had never heard one that sounded so much like a man asking for a way out. And now the whole city had heard it too, and the whole city was going to forget it by nightfall, because the whole city was busy asking a different question, the one Seraph had planted in three hundred thousand heads at once: who gets everything?

Six people had gone up the Tower carrying honest questions, and the world below them had already decided they were carrying a crown.

Rainbow raised his glass toward the empty street outside.

Behind him, the small screen above the back bar, which had been cycling the standard feed, stuttered. One frame of white. One word, in the voice that was not loud so much as everywhere, quiet now, almost to itself:

"Everything."

Then the feed came back, a vendor advertising boots, and the morning went on.

Rainbow drank, and went back to work.
